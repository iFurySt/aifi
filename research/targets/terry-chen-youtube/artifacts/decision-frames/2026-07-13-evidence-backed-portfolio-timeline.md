## Generalized Portfolio Draft For Ordinary Investors

This draft is deliberately conservative because Terry's transcript-backed path includes concentrated stock exposure, options, real estate operations, business income, and crypto-yield risk. Those are not automatically transferable to ordinary investors.

1. Runway and cash floor: keep enough cash or short-duration safe yield to avoid forced selling. Terry's own framework emphasizes failovers and instruments such as short-term Treasuries/IB01 before optionality.
2. Broad-market core: make a diversified equity core the default for ordinary investors. The `Q59J5roE5lM` transcript directly supports that investing in the broad market remains suitable for most people, partly because most active managers fail to beat the market and single-name volatility is behaviorally hard.
3. Optional technology tilt: if an investor has a reasoned belief that technology will lead the next cycle, a partial QQQ-style allocation is a more transferable expression than jumping directly to concentrated single names.
4. Concentrated satellite: cap single-name exposure. Terry appears comfortable with Tesla and other high-conviction names, but the transcript-backed ordinary-investor rule is that active stock picking is not for everyone and requires volatility tolerance, process, and discipline.
5. Income overlay only after ownership discipline: covered calls can monetize holdings, but the transcript-backed process includes roll decisions, earnings avoidance, and position-aware tools. A beginner should not treat this as passive yield.
6. Real-estate/business sleeve: rental income and side-business income are portfolio components only when operations are understood or delegated well. They are not as liquid as public assets.
7. Crypto/yield sleeve: keep any crypto, stablecoin lending, or platform-yield exposure capped and separable from essential living capital. Treat platform failure as a real risk.
8. Human-capital engine: career skill, consulting, business building, and creator distribution are part of the financial moat because they create investable surplus and reduce dependency on one employer.

## Practice-First Learning Plan

1. Personal cash-flow map: list income streams, fixed costs, debt, tax obligations, and minimum runway. This is the base layer before security selection.
2. Index baseline: learn broad-market ETF behavior, drawdowns, contribution discipline, and rebalancing. Every active bet should be judged against this baseline.
3. One-stock deep dive: choose one company and write a thesis, failure case, valuation range, position cap, and exit rule. Do this before owning a large single-name position.
4. Options lab: paper-trade covered calls on a position you would be willing to keep or sell. Track assignment, roll rules, taxes, and missed-upside risk.
5. Real-estate cash-flow model: build a simple rental model with vacancy, repairs, manager fees, insurance, financing, taxes, and legal structure before buying anything.
6. Crypto custody and platform risk: learn wallet custody, exchange risk, stablecoin mechanics, lending/platform counterparty risk, and position sizing before chasing yield.
7. Portfolio policy: write down max allocation by sleeve, rebalancing triggers, income targets, and what happens when a holding, platform, job, or business income stream goes to zero.

# Peer Comparison Framework

## Peer Types

- Direct competitors: sell similar products to similar customers.
- Segment competitors: overlap with one important segment, not the whole company.
- Substitutes: solve the same customer problem differently.
- Suppliers and customers: affect bargaining power and margin structure.
- Benchmark companies: useful for valuation or operating model comparison, even
  if not direct competitors.

## Comparison Dimensions

| Dimension | Questions |
| --- | --- |
| Products | What does each company sell, and which workloads or use cases matter? |
| Customers | Who buys, and how concentrated are customers or channels? |
| Technology | What are the performance, roadmap, manufacturing, or IP advantages? |
| Economics | How do growth, gross margin, operating margin, capex, and cash flow differ? |
| Strategy | What is each company investing in, exiting, or defending? |
| Valuation | What expectations are embedded in market multiples? |
| Risks | What could change the competitive balance? |

## Semiconductor And Intel-Relevant Peer Map

Use when researching Intel-like targets:

- CPU and client compute: AMD, Apple, Qualcomm where relevant.
- Data center compute and accelerators: AMD, Nvidia, custom silicon providers.
- Foundry and manufacturing: TSMC, Samsung Foundry, GlobalFoundries.
- Semiconductor equipment and ecosystem dependencies: ASML, Applied Materials,
  Lam Research, KLA where process-node execution matters.
- Broad semiconductor benchmarks: SOX constituents or semiconductor ETFs.

## Anti-Patterns

- Do not compare Intel's foundry losses with Nvidia's fabless margins without
  explaining business-model differences.
- Do not treat market cap as competitive strength by itself.
- Do not assume current technology leadership lasts without roadmap evidence.
